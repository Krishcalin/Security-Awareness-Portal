# Security Awareness Training — Voice-Over Scripts

**20 slides · ~60 seconds each · ~145–155 words per slide (≈150 wpm delivery pace)**

Delivery notes: read at a calm, conversational pace. Pause briefly at each paragraph break. Where a script mentions the security team or helpdesk, substitute your organisation's actual contact details before recording.

---

## Slide 1 — Title: Security Awareness Training

Welcome. Over the next few minutes we're going to talk about something that touches every single person here — security.

Not the technical kind that lives inside firewalls and servers. The everyday kind that lives in your inbox, on your phone, at your desk, and in the small decisions you make without thinking twice.

You don't need a technical background to follow this. There's no jargon and no code. What you will get is a clear picture of how attackers actually try to reach us, what those attempts look like in real life, and the simple habits that stop them.

Here's the thing worth remembering before we start. Attackers rarely break in through technology anymore. They walk in through people — through a rushed click, a reused password, a helpful answer given to the wrong person.

That doesn't make you the weak link. It makes you the control that works. Let's begin.

---

## Slide 2 — Why Security Matters

Let's start with the honest answer to a fair question: why should this matter to you?

Because the information you handle every day has value to someone else. Customer records. Financial data. Operational systems. Your own login credentials. To an attacker, all of it is either something to sell, something to hold hostage, or a door into something bigger.

And the consequences aren't abstract. A single compromised account can shut down services people depend on. It can expose personal data belonging to customers and colleagues. It can trigger regulatory action, financial loss, and damage to a reputation that took decades to build.

But here's the part that often gets missed. Security isn't only the organisation's problem. The same habits that protect your work also protect your bank account, your family photos, your identity.

Good security at work makes you safer everywhere. That's why it matters.

---

## Slide 3 — Phishing Emails

Phishing is still the single most common way attacks begin — and it works because it doesn't look like an attack. It looks like an email.

Look at three things every time.

First, the sender. It may display a name you recognise, but the actual address behind it is often subtly wrong — an extra letter, a different domain, a public mail service where a company address should be.

Second, the tone. Phishing runs on urgency. Your account will be suspended. Payment is overdue. Act within the hour. That pressure is deliberate. It's designed to stop you thinking and start you clicking.

Third, the links. Hover your cursor over any link before you click and read where it actually goes. The text on screen and the real destination are often two different places.

Slow down. Urgency is the attacker's tool, not yours.

---

## Slide 4 — Phishing Red Flags

Let's turn that into a quick mental checklist you can run in about ten seconds.

Suspicious sender. Was this expected? Do I actually know this person, and is the address genuinely theirs? A familiar display name is not proof of anything.

Urgent tone. Am I being pushed to act immediately, or warned that something bad happens if I don't? Genuine business rarely arrives with a countdown attached.

Strange links. Does the destination match the sender? A message claiming to be from your bank shouldn't be sending you to an unrelated domain. Hover first, click second.

Unexpected attachments. Invoices you never ordered. Delivery notices for parcels you're not expecting. Documents that demand you enable content before they'll open.

Any one of these deserves a second look. Two or more together — stop, don't click, and report it.

---

## Slide 5 — Password Best Practices

Passwords are still the front door to almost everything you use, so let's cover what actually works.

Length beats complexity. A long passphrase of several unrelated words is far harder to crack than a short password stuffed with symbols — and much easier to remember. Aim for at least twelve to sixteen characters.

Never reuse passwords across accounts. This is the big one. Breaches happen constantly, and when credentials leak, attackers immediately try that same combination everywhere else. One reused password can unlock a dozen accounts.

Use a password manager. It generates strong, unique passwords for every site and remembers them all, so you only need to recall one strong master passphrase. That's not a shortcut — it's the practical way to do this properly.

And never share your password. Not with a colleague, not with IT, not with anyone who asks. Legitimate teams never need it.

---

## Slide 6 — Multi-Factor Authentication

Even a strong password can be stolen. Multi-factor authentication is what makes that theft useless.

The idea is simple. Instead of proving who you are with one thing, you prove it with two. Something you know — your password. And something you have — your phone, an authenticator app, or a hardware key.

An attacker on the other side of the world may have your password. But without that second factor sitting in your pocket, they still can't get in.

Two things to keep in mind. Prefer an authenticator app or a security key over text messages where you have the choice — SMS codes can be intercepted.

And treat unexpected prompts as a warning. If your phone buzzes with an approval request you didn't trigger, don't tap approve to make it stop. Deny it, and report it. Someone is already using your password.

## Slide 7 — Safe Web Browsing

The web is where a lot of trouble starts, but a few habits keep you clear of most of it.

Check the address bar. Read the actual domain name carefully, especially the part just before the first slash. Attackers register lookalike domains with swapped letters, extra words, or unusual endings, and they can look convincing at a glance.

Look for the padlock and HTTPS — but understand what it means. It tells you the connection is encrypted. It does not tell you the site is honest. Criminals get certificates too.

Be sceptical of pop-ups. Warnings that your device is infected, prizes you've supposedly won, urgent prompts to install a plugin or update — these are bait. Close the tab; don't interact with the pop-up.

And avoid downloading software from unofficial sources. Free rarely is.

---

## Slide 8 — Social Engineering

Social engineering is the art of hacking people instead of systems. And it works because it targets instincts that normally serve you well — helpfulness, respect for authority, and the wish to avoid conflict.

Pretexting is the invented backstory. Someone calls claiming to be an auditor, a vendor engineer, or a new colleague on a deadline. The story is plausible, detailed, and completely false.

Impersonation is the borrowed identity. They pose as your manager, your IT team, or a senior executive — usually someone you'd hesitate to question.

Manipulation is the pressure applied on top. Flattery, urgency, sympathy, or mild intimidation, all aimed at getting you to skip the step where you verify.

Your defence is simple and it never gets old. Verify independently. Hang up, look up the number yourself, and call back. Genuine requests survive verification.

---

## Slide 9 — Vishing and Smishing

Phishing doesn't stay in your inbox. It comes by phone and by text too.

Vishing is voice phishing — a live call. The caller may claim to be from your bank, your IT helpdesk, a government office, or a delivery company. They often know a few real details about you, which makes the rest sound true. They may spoof a genuine number on your screen. Caller ID proves nothing.

Smishing is the text-message version. A short SMS or WhatsApp message with a link. A failed delivery. A blocked payment. A refund waiting for you.

The rules are the same in both cases. Never share passwords, one-time codes, or card details with someone who contacted you. Never let a caller talk you into installing remote-access software.

Hang up, or ignore the text. Then reach out yourself using a number you already trust.

## Slide 10 — What Is Ransomware?

Ransomware is malicious software with one purpose: to take your data hostage.

It usually arrives quietly — through a phishing attachment, a malicious link, or a compromised remote-access account. Once inside, it starts encrypting files. Documents, spreadsheets, databases, shared drives. Anything it can reach.

Then the demand appears on screen. Pay us, in cryptocurrency, within a deadline, and we'll give you the key.

Modern ransomware goes further. Before locking anything, attackers often copy your data out first. So even organisations with perfect backups face a second threat — publish or sell what we stole unless you pay. That's called double extortion.

The impact is immediate and physical. Systems stop. Operations halt. Teams sit idle. Recovery is measured in days and weeks, not hours.

Which is exactly why the everyday habits in this training matter so much. Prevention is dramatically cheaper than recovery.

---

## Slide 11 — Ransomware: What To Do

If you ever suspect ransomware on your machine, three actions matter — in this order.

One: disconnect from the network. Unplug the network cable and switch off Wi-Fi immediately. This limits how far the encryption can spread to shared drives and other systems. But do not power the machine off — valuable evidence lives in memory, and shutting down can destroy it.

Two: report immediately. Call the security team or the helpdesk straight away. Minutes genuinely matter here. And please — never delay because you're worried about being blamed. Fast reporting is what limits the damage, and nobody is penalised for raising the alarm.

Three: do not pay, and do not negotiate. That decision isn't yours to carry, and it isn't yours to make alone. Payment funds the next attack and offers no guarantee of recovery.

Disconnect. Report. Step back and let the response team work.

## Slide 12 — Physical Security

Not every attack arrives over the network. Some walk through the door.

A clean desk is a security control. Documents left out, notes with credentials on them, unlocked drawers — all of it is readable by anyone passing your desk, including cleaners, contractors and visitors. Lock papers away, and lock your screen every time you step away. Every time.

Tailgating is when someone follows you through a secured door without badging in themselves. It feels rude to stop them — that's precisely why it works. Holding a door open for a stranger is not politeness; it's an access-control bypass. Let the door close, and ask them to badge in.

And wear your badge visibly. It's how everyone else knows you belong here — and how you spot someone who doesn't.

If you see someone unfamiliar in a restricted area, ask, or report it.

## Slide 13 — Remote Work Security

Working away from the office removes a lot of protection you never noticed you had. Here's how to put it back.

Use the VPN. When you're outside the office, it's what encrypts your traffic and keeps access to company systems controlled. Connect first, then start working.

Lock your screen — always. At home, in a café, at a co-working desk. It takes one second and prevents a whole category of problems.

Secure your home Wi-Fi. Change the default router password, use WPA2 or WPA3 encryption, and keep the router firmware updated. Your home network is now part of the company's attack surface.

And mind your surroundings. Be aware of who can see your screen or hear your calls in shared spaces. Confidential conversations deserve a closed door.

Same standards as the office — just applied by you.

## Slide 14 — Public Wi-Fi Risks

Free Wi-Fi is convenient. It's also uncontrolled, and you have no idea who else is on it or who set it up.

The main risk is interception. On an open network, an attacker sitting nearby can potentially watch unencrypted traffic or position themselves between you and the site you're visiting.

There's a second trick worth knowing: the fake hotspot. Attackers set up a network with a name that looks official — the café's name, the airport's name, something close enough. You connect, and everything you do passes through their equipment. So verify the exact network name with staff before joining.

Two rules keep you safe. Always use the VPN on public Wi-Fi. And avoid sensitive tasks — banking, payments, confidential work — unless that VPN is running.

When in doubt, use your mobile hotspot instead. It's far safer than any open network.

## Slide 15 — USB and Removable Media

A USB stick looks harmless. It can be anything but.

Never plug in a device you didn't buy or weren't issued. Found drives are a genuine attack technique — left in car parks, reception areas, or corridors, relying on curiosity to do the rest. Some are simply infected. Others are engineered to imitate a keyboard and type malicious commands the moment they're connected.

If you must use removable media, scan it first. And keep to devices approved and issued by the organisation, ideally with encryption enabled — so if it's lost, the data on it stays protected.

That last point cuts both ways. Copying company data onto a personal drive creates risk even when your intentions are good. Once it leaves controlled systems, it can't be tracked, protected, or wiped.

If you find an unknown drive, don't test it. Hand it to the security team.

---

## Slide 16 — Data Classification

Not all information carries the same risk, so it shouldn't get the same handling. That's what classification is for.

Public data is approved for anyone to see — published material, marketing content, information already in the open. Little risk if it spreads.

Internal data is for people inside the organisation. Process documents, internal announcements, routine operational information. Not damaging on its own, but not for outside eyes either.

Confidential data is the material that causes real harm if exposed. Customer and employee personal data, financial records, contracts, credentials, system designs, anything covered by regulation.

Two practical rules follow from this. Know the classification of what you're handling before you send, share, or store it. And apply the right controls — encryption, restricted access, approved channels only.

The most common breach isn't a hack. It's confidential information sent to the wrong recipient.

---

## Slide 17 — Report an Incident

Reporting is the single most valuable thing you can do, so let's make it easy.

When to report? Whenever something feels wrong. A suspicious email. A link you clicked and immediately regretted. A device that's gone missing. A login alert you didn't trigger. Unusual system behaviour. A stranger in a secured area. You don't need to be sure — uncertainty is exactly when to report.

How to report? Use the channels your organisation provides — the phishing report button, the helpdesk, or the security hotline. Report first, before trying to fix it yourself.

Who to contact? Know your security team's number before you need it. Save it in your phone today.

And one thing above all: report fast, even if you think you made the mistake. Early reporting turns a potential crisis into a routine event. Nobody is punished for speaking up.

---

## Slide 18 — Insider Threats

Not every risk comes from outside. Some originate within — and most of them aren't malicious at all.

Accidental insiders make ordinary human errors. An email sent to the wrong address. A file shared with a link set too widely. A laptop left in a taxi. No bad intent, real consequences.

Negligent insiders know the rules and work around them. Sharing a login to save time. Storing company files on a personal cloud account. Ignoring updates. Convenience wins, protection loses.

Malicious insiders are rare, but real — someone deliberately misusing legitimate access for personal gain or out of grievance.

What helps is straightforward. Take only the access you need. Follow the process even when it's slower. And if you notice something genuinely concerning — unusual data being copied, access being misused — raise it discreetly with the security team.

---

## Slide 19 — Your Role in Security

Everything we've covered comes down to three habits.

Stay alert. Most attacks announce themselves if you're paying attention — the odd sender address, the unexpected urgency, the request that doesn't quite fit your normal process. Awareness costs you seconds and saves everyone days.

Follow policies. They exist because someone, somewhere, learned the lesson the hard way. The VPN, the approved tools, the access requests, the classification rules — they're not bureaucracy, they're accumulated experience. And if a policy actively makes your job impossible, say so, so it can be fixed properly rather than quietly bypassed.

Speak up. Report what looks wrong. Ask when you're unsure. Challenge the unfamiliar face at a secured door. Tell us when you've clicked something you shouldn't have.

Technology stops a great deal. People stop the rest. That's your role, and it genuinely matters.

---

## Slide 20 — Key Takeaways

Let's close with what's worth carrying out of this session.

Think before you click. Verify the sender, hover over links, and treat urgency as a warning sign rather than an instruction.

Use strong, unique passwords and turn on multi-factor authentication everywhere it's offered. A password manager makes both of these effortless.

Protect your devices and your data. Lock your screen, use the VPN, avoid unknown USB drives, and handle confidential information through approved channels only.

And report anything suspicious, immediately — even, and especially, when you think the mistake was yours.

None of this requires you to become a security expert. It asks for a few seconds of attention at the right moments, and the confidence to ask a question when something feels off.

You now have both. Thank you for your time — and welcome to the team that keeps this organisation secure.
