## Slide 1 — Security Awareness Training

Welcome. Over the next few minutes we're going to talk about something that touches every person here — security.

Not the technical kind that lives inside firewalls and servers. The everyday kind that lives in your inbox, on your phone, at your desk, and in the small decisions you make without thinking twice.

You don't need a technical background to follow this. There's no jargon and no code. What you will get is a clear picture of how attackers actually try to reach us, what those attempts look like in real life, and the simple habits that stop them.

Consider what these have in common: eighty-one million dollars stolen from Bangladesh's central bank. A fuel pipeline serving America's east coast shut down for six days. The lights going out across two provinces of Ukraine in the middle of winter. In every one of those cases, the entry point was ordinary. An email. A password. A remote-access account nobody had switched off.

Here's what's worth remembering before we start. Attackers rarely break in through technology anymore. They walk in through people — through a rushed click, a reused password, a helpful answer given to the wrong person.

That doesn't make you the weak link. It makes you the control that works. Let's begin.

## Slide 2 — Why Security Matters

Let's start with the honest answer to a fair question: why should this matter to you?

Because the information you handle every day has value to someone else. Customer records. Financial data. Operational systems. Your own login credentials. To an attacker, all of it is either something to sell, something to hold hostage, or a door into something bigger.

In February 2016, attackers who had spent months quietly inside Bangladesh Bank's network issued fraudulent SWIFT payment instructions and moved eighty-one million dollars out of the country. They had even tampered with the bank's printer so the confirmation slips wouldn't appear. They attempted close to a billion dollars in total. What stopped the rest wasn't a security tool — it was a bank employee at a correspondent bank noticing a spelling mistake in one of the instructions.

The consequences aren't abstract. A single compromised account can halt services people depend on, expose personal data belonging to customers and colleagues, and trigger regulatory action and lasting reputational damage.

And it isn't only the organisation's problem. The same habits that protect your work protect your bank account, your family photos, your identity.

Good security at work makes you safer everywhere. That's why it matters.

## Slide 3 — Phishing Emails

Phishing is still the most common way attacks begin — and it works because it doesn't look like an attack. It looks like an email.

Look at three things every time.

First, the sender. It may display a name you recognise, but the actual address behind it is often subtly wrong — an extra letter, a different domain, a public mail service where a company address should be.

Second, the tone. Phishing runs on urgency. Your account will be suspended. Payment is overdue. Act within the hour. That pressure is deliberate. It's designed to stop you thinking and start you clicking.

Third, the links. Hover your cursor over any link and read where it actually goes. The text on screen and the real destination are often two different places.

In December 2015, attackers took down parts of Ukraine's power grid — the first confirmed cyberattack to cause a blackout. Thirty substations went dark and around two hundred and thirty thousand people lost power. It began months earlier with spear-phishing emails carrying an infected Office attachment. Someone opened the file and enabled macros. That single click gave attackers the foothold they used to steal credentials and eventually operate the breakers themselves.

Slow down. Urgency is the attacker's tool, not yours.

## Slide 4 — Phishing Red Flags

Let's turn that into a mental checklist you can run in about ten seconds.

Suspicious sender. Was this expected? Do I know this person, and is the address genuinely theirs? A familiar display name is not proof of anything.

Urgent tone. Am I being pushed to act immediately, or warned that something bad happens if I don't? Genuine business rarely arrives with a countdown attached.

Strange links. Does the destination match the sender? A message claiming to be from your bank shouldn't send you to an unrelated domain. Hover first, click second.

Unexpected attachments. Invoices you never ordered. Delivery notices for parcels you aren't expecting. Documents demanding you enable content before they'll open.

Two patterns show up constantly in Indian workplaces. The first is the fake IT notice: "Your mailbox password expires today — click here to keep your account active." The link opens a perfect copy of your login page, and your credentials go straight to the attacker. The second is invoice fraud: a supplier you genuinely deal with emails to say their bank account has changed, and the next payment goes to a criminal. For any payment-detail change, stop and verify by phone on a number you already hold — never a number in the email.

Any one red flag deserves a second look. Two or more — stop, don't click, and report it.

## Slide 5 — Password Best Practices

Passwords are still the front door to almost everything you use, so let's cover what actually works.

Length beats complexity. A long passphrase of several unrelated words is far harder to crack than a short password stuffed with symbols — and much easier to remember. Aim for at least twelve to sixteen characters.

Never reuse passwords across accounts. This is the big one. Breaches happen constantly, and when credentials leak, attackers immediately try that same combination everywhere else.

In May 2021, the Colonial Pipeline — which carries nearly half the fuel consumed on America's east coast — shut down for six days. Petrol stations ran dry across several states. The entry point was a single password on an old remote-access account that nobody had disabled and that had no second factor. Investigators found that password in a batch of leaked credentials from an unrelated breach, which suggests it had been reused somewhere else. One reused password, on one forgotten account, stopped a pipeline.

Use a password manager. It generates strong, unique passwords for every site and remembers them all, so you only need to recall one strong master passphrase. That's not a shortcut — it's the practical way to do this properly.

And never share your password. Not with a colleague, not with IT, not with anyone who asks.

## Slide 6 — Multi-Factor Authentication

Even a strong password can be stolen. Multi-factor authentication is what makes that theft useless.

The idea is simple. Instead of proving who you are with one thing, you prove it with two. Something you know — your password. And something you have — your phone, an authenticator app, or a hardware key.

An attacker on the other side of the world may have your password. But without that second factor in your pocket, they still can't get in.

Return to Colonial Pipeline for a moment. The compromised account had a password but no second factor. Investigators were explicit that multi-factor authentication on that one legacy account would very likely have stopped the whole incident. It is genuinely that decisive.

Two things to keep in mind. Prefer an authenticator app or a security key over text messages where you have the choice — SMS codes can be intercepted or redirected through SIM-swap fraud.

And treat unexpected prompts as a warning. Attackers now deliberately spam approval requests at all hours, hoping you'll tap "approve" just to make the buzzing stop. That technique has breached major companies. If a prompt appears that you didn't trigger, deny it and report it. Someone already has your password.

## Slide 7 — Safe Web Browsing

The web is where a lot of trouble starts, but a few habits keep you clear of most of it.

Check the address bar. Read the actual domain name carefully, especially the part just before the first slash. Attackers register lookalike domains with swapped letters, extra words, or unusual endings, and they look convincing at a glance.

Look for the padlock and HTTPS — but understand what it means. It tells you the connection is encrypted. It does not tell you the site is honest. Criminals get certificates too.

Be sceptical of pop-ups. Warnings that your device is infected, prizes you've won, urgent prompts to install a plugin — these are bait. Close the tab; don't interact with the pop-up.

Attackers targeting the energy sector have used a technique called a watering hole: rather than attacking a utility directly, they compromise a website that utility staff visit often — an industry association, a trade publication, an equipment supplier's site — and plant malware there. You visit a site you have used for years, and it infects you. Campaigns against energy companies in the US and Europe worked exactly this way.

And avoid downloading software from unofficial sources. Free rarely is.

## Slide 8 — Social Engineering

Social engineering is the art of hacking people instead of systems. It works because it targets instincts that normally serve you well — helpfulness, respect for authority, and the wish to avoid conflict.

Pretexting is the invented backstory. Someone calls claiming to be an auditor, a vendor engineer, or a new colleague on a deadline. The story is plausible, detailed, and completely false.

Impersonation is the borrowed identity. They pose as your manager, your IT team, or a senior executive — usually someone you'd hesitate to question.

Manipulation is the pressure applied on top. Flattery, urgency, sympathy, or mild intimidation, all aimed at getting you to skip the step where you verify.

In September 2023, attackers brought a major international hotel and casino group to a standstill for days. No malware was needed for the first step. They found an employee's details on LinkedIn, telephoned the IT service desk, impersonated that employee convincingly enough to have the credentials reset, and walked in. One phone call. The eventual cost ran into hundreds of millions of dollars.

Your defence is simple and never gets old. Verify independently. Hang up, look up the number yourself, and call back. Genuine requests survive verification.

## Slide 9 — Vishing and Smishing

Phishing doesn't stay in your inbox. It comes by phone and by text too.

Vishing is voice phishing — a live call. The caller may claim to be from your bank, your IT helpdesk, a government office, or a delivery company. They often know a few real details about you, which makes the rest sound true. They may spoof a genuine number on your screen. Caller ID proves nothing.

Smishing is the text-message version. A short SMS or WhatsApp message with a link. A failed delivery. A blocked payment. A refund waiting for you.

One scam is worth naming because it impersonates our own industry. Across India, people receive an SMS reading roughly: "Dear customer, your electricity will be disconnected tonight at 9:30 pm because your previous bill was not updated. Please contact our officer immediately." Victims call the number, are talked into installing a remote-access app to "complete the payment," and their bank account is emptied. Our customers receive these. So do we. If anyone asks you to install an app so they can see your screen, that call is an attack.

The rules are the same in both cases. Never share passwords, one-time codes, or card details with someone who contacted you. Hang up, then reach out yourself on a number you already trust.

## Slide 10 — What Is Ransomware?

Ransomware is malicious software with one purpose: to take your data hostage.

It usually arrives quietly — through a phishing attachment, a malicious link, or a compromised remote-access account. Once inside, it starts encrypting files. Documents, spreadsheets, databases, shared drives. Anything it can reach. Then the demand appears: pay us, in cryptocurrency, within a deadline, and we'll give you the key.

Modern ransomware goes further. Before locking anything, attackers usually copy your data out first. So even organisations with perfect backups face a second threat — publish or sell what we stole unless you pay. That's called double extortion.

In March 2019, the Norwegian aluminium producer Norsk Hydro was hit. Twenty-two thousand computers across forty countries were encrypted in hours. Staff ran plants from paper printouts and whiteboards; some smelters were operated manually to stop the molten metal solidifying. Recovery cost around seventy million dollars. And in November 2022, AIIMS Delhi lost its systems for close to two weeks — appointments, admissions and reports all reverted to paper across one of India's largest hospitals.

The impact is immediate and physical. Systems stop, operations halt, recovery is measured in weeks. Which is exactly why the everyday habits in this training matter. Prevention is dramatically cheaper than recovery.

---

## Slide 11 — Ransomware: What To Do

If you ever suspect ransomware on your machine, three actions matter — in this order.

One: disconnect from the network. Unplug the network cable and switch off Wi-Fi immediately. This limits how far the encryption can spread to shared drives and other systems. But do not power the machine off — valuable evidence lives in memory, and shutting down destroys it.

Two: report immediately. Call the security team or the helpdesk straight away. Minutes genuinely matter. And never delay because you're worried about being blamed. Fast reporting is what limits the damage, and nobody is penalised for raising the alarm.

Three: do not pay, and do not negotiate. That decision isn't yours to carry alone.

Two contrasting responses are instructive. Colonial Pipeline paid roughly four point four million dollars — and then found the decryption tool the criminals supplied was so slow that they restored from their own backups anyway. Norsk Hydro refused to pay, rebuilt from backups, and did something unusual: they published daily updates, held open press briefings, and told the world exactly what had happened. They were widely praised, kept their customers' trust, and recovered their reputation intact. Transparency turned out to be a better strategy than payment.

Disconnect. Report. Step back and let the response team work.

## Slide 12 — Physical Security

Not every attack arrives over the network. Some walk through the door.

A clean desk is a security control. Documents left out, notes with credentials on them, unlocked drawers — all readable by anyone passing your desk, including cleaners, contractors and visitors. Lock papers away, and lock your screen every time you step away. Every time.

Tailgating is when someone follows you through a secured door without badging in. It feels rude to stop them — which is precisely why it works. Holding a door open for a stranger is not politeness; it's an access-control bypass. Let the door close, and ask them to badge in.

Penetration testers hired to test physical security report success rates that should worry all of us. The standard techniques are embarrassingly simple: carry a large box so someone opens the door for you; wear a high-visibility jacket and carry a clipboard; arrive at the smoking area at shift change and walk back in with the group; or simply say "I've left my badge at my desk." In test after test, these work — not because staff are careless, but because people are decent and don't want to cause a scene.

Wear your badge visibly. If you see someone unfamiliar in a restricted area, ask, or report it.

## Slide 13 — Remote Work Security

Working away from the office removes protection you never noticed you had. Here's how to put it back.

Use the VPN. When you're outside the office, it's what encrypts your traffic and keeps access to company systems controlled. Connect first, then start working.

Lock your screen — always. At home, in a café, at a co-working desk. One second, and it prevents a whole category of problems.

Secure your home Wi-Fi. Change the default router password, use WPA2 or WPA3 encryption, and keep the router firmware updated. Your home network is now part of the company's attack surface.

Remote access is not a small door — for many major breaches it has been the only door. The Colonial Pipeline shutdown started on a dormant VPN account. Attacks on water utilities have begun with remote-support tools left running with shared passwords. The pattern repeats because remote access is convenient, easily forgotten, and rarely reviewed. If you have access you no longer use, tell IT so it can be removed. Unused access is pure risk with no benefit.

And mind your surroundings. Be aware of who can see your screen or hear your calls in shared spaces. Confidential conversations deserve a closed door.

## Slide 14 — Public Wi-Fi Risks

Free Wi-Fi is convenient. It's also uncontrolled, and you have no idea who else is on it or who set it up.

The main risk is interception. On an open network, an attacker sitting nearby can potentially watch unencrypted traffic or position themselves between you and the site you're visiting.

There's a second trick worth knowing: the evil twin. An attacker sets up a hotspot with a name that looks official — the café's name, the airport's name, the hotel's name, something close enough that nobody looks twice. Your phone connects, often automatically if it has seen a similar name before, and everything you do passes through their equipment. The hardware to do this costs less than a decent dinner and fits in a pocket. Journalists and researchers demonstrate it at airports and conferences routinely, harvesting credentials in minutes. So verify the exact network name with staff before joining — and turn off automatic connection to open networks on your phone.

Two rules keep you safe. Always use the VPN on public Wi-Fi. And avoid sensitive tasks — banking, payments, confidential work — unless that VPN is running.

When in doubt, use your mobile hotspot. It's far safer than any open network.

## Slide 15 — USB and Removable Media

A USB stick looks harmless. It can be anything but.

Never plug in a device you didn't buy or weren't issued. Found drives are a genuine attack technique — left in car parks, reception areas, or corridors, relying on curiosity to do the rest. Some are simply infected. Others are engineered to imitate a keyboard and type malicious commands the moment they're connected.

The most consequential example in history is Stuxnet. Around 2010, malware was discovered inside Iran's uranium enrichment facility at Natanz — a site with no internet connection at all, deliberately air-gapped from the outside world. The malware got in on a USB drive, carried across that gap by a person. It then quietly altered the speed of the centrifuges while showing normal readings on the operators' screens, and destroyed roughly a thousand of them before anyone understood why. An air gap is not a wall. It is a wall with a door, and USB devices are the door.

If you must use removable media, scan it first, and use only approved, encrypted, organisation-issued devices. Copying company data onto a personal drive creates risk even with the best intentions.

If you find an unknown drive, don't test it. Hand it to the security team.

## Slide 16 — Data Classification

Not all information carries the same risk, so it shouldn't get the same handling. That's what classification is for.

Public data is approved for anyone to see — published material, marketing content, information already in the open.

Internal data is for people inside the organisation. Process documents, internal announcements, routine operational information.

Confidential data is the material that causes real harm if exposed. Customer and employee personal data, financial records, contracts, credentials, system designs, network diagrams, anything covered by regulation.

For a utility, one category deserves particular care: engineering and design documentation. Network diagrams, control room layouts, equipment inventories, vendor lists, single-line diagrams. None of it is personal data, so it feels low-risk — but to an attacker planning an operation it is a map. In 2026 it was reported that roughly nineteen thousand documents relating to the Kudankulam nuclear project appeared on the dark web, apparently through a contractor rather than the plant itself: engineering drawings, control room layouts, supplier lists. The reactors were never touched. The blueprint still leaked.

Two practical rules. Know the classification of what you're handling before you send, share or store it. And apply the right controls — encryption, restricted access, approved channels only.

The most common breach isn't a hack. It's confidential information sent to the wrong recipient.

## Slide 17 — Report an Incident

Reporting is the single most valuable thing you can do, so let's make it easy.

When to report? Whenever something feels wrong. A suspicious email. A link you clicked and immediately regretted. A device that's gone missing. A login alert you didn't trigger. Unusual system behaviour. A stranger in a secured area. You don't need to be sure — uncertainty is exactly when to report.

How to report? Use the channels the organisation provides — the phishing report button, the helpdesk, or the security hotline. Report first, before trying to fix it yourself.

Who to contact? Know your security team's number before you need it. Save it in your phone today.

Attackers understand the value of delay. During the 2015 Ukraine grid attack, at the same moment they were opening breakers, they launched a flood of automated calls at the utility's customer service centre — jamming the lines so customers couldn't report the outage and operators couldn't work out what was happening. They also wiped the operators' workstations on the way out. Every one of those moves was designed to buy time by delaying detection. Fast reporting is how you take that time back.

Report fast, even if you think you made the mistake. Early reporting turns a potential crisis into a routine event. Nobody is punished for speaking up.

## Slide 18 — Insider Threats

Not every risk comes from outside. Some originate within — and most are not malicious at all.

Accidental insiders make ordinary human errors. An email to the wrong address. A file shared with a link set too widely. A laptop left in a taxi. No bad intent, real consequences.

Negligent insiders know the rules and work around them. Sharing a login to save time. Storing company files on a personal cloud account. Ignoring updates. Convenience wins, protection loses.

Malicious insiders are rare, but real — someone deliberately misusing legitimate access for personal gain or out of grievance.

A US rural water district discovered the risk in the plainest way possible. A former employee, whose remote access had never been revoked after he left, logged in and shut down the plant's cleaning and disinfection processes. No hacking was involved. No password was cracked. The account simply still worked. That is why the leavers process — collecting badges, disabling accounts, revoking VPN and vendor access on the last working day — is a security control and not an administrative formality.

What helps is straightforward. Take only the access you need. Follow the process even when it's slower. And if something genuinely concerns you, raise it discreetly with the security team.

## Slide 19 — Your Role in Security

Everything we've covered comes down to three habits.

Stay alert. Most attacks announce themselves if you're paying attention — the odd sender address, the unexpected urgency, the request that doesn't fit your normal process. Awareness costs seconds and saves everyone days.

Follow policies. They exist because someone, somewhere, learned the lesson the hard way. The VPN, the approved tools, the access requests, the classification rules — that's accumulated experience, not bureaucracy. And if a policy actively makes your job impossible, say so, so it can be fixed properly rather than quietly bypassed.

Speak up. Report what looks wrong. Ask when you're unsure. Challenge the unfamiliar face at a secured door. Tell us when you've clicked something you shouldn't have.

One image from the Ukraine attack captures this better than any statistic. An operator sat at his console and watched the mouse cursor move across the screen on its own — travelling to a breaker, selecting it, opening it. He grabbed the mouse and fought for control. He lost, and was then locked out of his own machine. But he was the detection system. No sensor raised that alarm. A person did, because he knew what normal looked like.

Technology stops a great deal. People stop the rest.

## Slide 20 — IT and OT Security

Welcome to the second part of this programme. Everything so far applied to any organisation anywhere. What follows is specific to us — to the business of generating and distributing electricity.

We'll be working with two worlds. IT — the systems that run the business: email, billing, HR, reporting. And OT, operational technology — the systems that run the grid: control rooms, substations, protection relays, SCADA.

Both matter. But they fail differently. When IT breaks, data is at risk. When OT breaks, power is at risk — and behind that sit hospitals, water pumping, traffic signals, and every home on our network.

This is no longer a hypothetical branch of security. Ukraine's grid has been attacked twice with real blackouts. India's regional and state load despatch centres have been found carrying implanted malware. In December 2025, more than thirty wind, solar and combined heat and power sites in Poland were hit in a single coordinated operation that physically damaged remote terminal units. OT attacks have moved from research papers to incident reports.

We'll also cover the CEA Cyber Security Regulations, which set out what every power utility in India must now do, and what that means for your daily work.

Whether you sit in an office or a switchyard, this section is for you.

## Slide 21 — What is OT Security?

Let's define both terms clearly, because this distinction shapes everything that follows.

IT — information technology — handles data. Emails, documents, databases, business applications. Its job is to keep information confidential, accurate and available.

OT — operational technology — handles the physical world. Sensors reading voltage, current and temperature. Controllers opening and closing breakers. Control room screens showing the live state of the network. Its job is to keep equipment running safely and predictably.

The simplest way to hold it: IT keeps data safe. OT keeps machines safe.

Stuxnet, discovered in 2010, was the moment this stopped being theoretical. It was the first malware built not to steal information but to break machinery — and it succeeded, destroying around a thousand centrifuges in Iran. What makes it instructive for us is the deception. While the equipment was being driven to destruction, the malware replayed earlier, normal-looking readings to the operators' screens. The people watching saw everything was fine. It wasn't. In OT, an attacker who controls your instrumentation controls your understanding of reality.

That changes the priority order. In IT we usually protect confidentiality first. In OT, availability and safety come first — because a control system that stops, or lies, has immediate physical consequences for equipment, for supply, and for people standing next to that equipment.

## Slide 22 — Why the Power Sector is a Target

Why would anyone attack a power utility? Three reasons.

First, we are critical infrastructure. Electricity underpins everything else — hospitals, water, transport, communications, industry. That makes the grid strategically valuable to attackers who want leverage rather than money.

Second, the impact is large-scale and immediate. An attack on many sectors means data is lost. An attack here means supply is lost, affecting an entire licence area at once.

Third, our systems are increasingly connected. Smart meters, remote substations, vendor support links, digital protection devices, IT and OT integration. Every connection adds capability — and a possible path inward.

The record is clear. December 2015: Ukraine, thirty substations opened remotely, two hundred and thirty thousand customers dark for up to six hours. December 2016: Kyiv, a fifth of the capital loses power to purpose-built malware that spoke industrial protocols natively. 2021 and 2022: security researchers report malware implanted in Indian regional and state load despatch centres — the bodies that balance the grid in real time. The stated goal was not disruption but pre-positioning: getting in quietly and waiting. December 2025: coordinated destructive attacks on Polish energy facilities.

Attackers range from criminal ransomware crews to state-sponsored teams who establish access and simply wait. India is squarely in scope — which is precisely why the regulations exist.

## Slide 23 — CEA Cyber Security Regulations

Which brings us to regulation.

India's Central Electricity Authority has issued the Cyber Security in Power Sector Regulations — a binding framework covering every entity in the power value chain: generation, transmission, distribution and load despatch.

They matter because they move cyber security from good practice to legal obligation. This is not guidance we may choose to adopt. It is a mandate, with defined roles, defined timelines, and consequences for non-compliance.

It's worth understanding why India legislated. In September 2019, malware was found on a computer on the administrative network of the Kudankulam nuclear plant, reaching a facility most people assumed was untouchable. From 2020 onwards, researchers documented sustained intrusions into load despatch centres across the country. The regulations are a direct response to that evidence: the state concluded that the security of the national grid was too important to leave to each operator's discretion.

The regulations span governance — who is accountable. Technical controls — how systems must be protected and separated. Incident reporting — what must be reported, to whom, and how quickly. Supply chain — what we require of our vendors. And people — training and awareness for everyone who touches these systems.

That last point is why you're watching this. Awareness training is a regulatory requirement, and your completion is part of our evidence.

---

## Slide 24 — CEA Key Requirements

Five requirements worth recognising.

One: appoint a CISO. A designated, accountable senior officer for cyber security, with a nominated alternate, both notified to the authorities. Accountability is named, not shared.

Two: conduct cyber audits. Regular assessments by empanelled auditors covering both IT and OT, with findings tracked to closure.

Three: network segmentation. Operational systems must be separated from business networks and from the internet, with controlled and monitored crossing points.

Four: incident reporting within six hours of noticing a cyber incident.

Five: employee awareness training, refreshed regularly, with records maintained.

Focus on that six-hour clock, because it's the one that touches you. Six hours is not long. It has to cover you noticing something, telling the security team, the team confirming it's real, gathering enough detail to describe it, and filing with the authorities. If you sit on an observation until the end of your shift, you have consumed most of the window before anyone else even knows. In the Ukraine attack the operators recognised something was wrong within minutes — and even then the attackers had jammed the phone lines to slow the alarm. Speed of reporting is a control in its own right.

Four of these are the organisation's job. The fifth, and that clock, depend on you.

## Slide 25 — IT vs OT: Key Differences

Understanding the differences explains why OT carries its own rules.

Lifespan. An office laptop is replaced every three to five years. A protection relay or controller may run for fifteen or twenty. Much of our OT estate predates modern security design entirely.

Patching. IT systems patch monthly, often automatically. OT patching needs a planned outage, vendor validation and formal approval — because an untested update on a live control system can trip equipment.

Priority. IT protects data first. OT protects availability and safety first.

Protocols. OT speaks industrial languages — Modbus, DNP3, IEC 61850 — many designed decades ago with no authentication and no encryption, on the assumption that the network was isolated.

Recovery differs most of all. When IT is hit, you rebuild from backups. When OT is hit, you may have nothing to rebuild onto. In Ukraine the attackers overwrote the firmware on the serial-to-Ethernet converters at the substations, bricking them permanently. The utility could no longer operate those breakers remotely at all. For months afterwards, engineers drove to substations and switched them by hand. You cannot restore damaged field hardware from a backup tape.

So never apply IT habits to OT systems. Follow the OT procedure.

## Slide 26 — SCADA and ICS Security

SCADA and industrial control systems are the nervous system of our operations. Protecting them rests on three practices.

Restrict remote access. Every connection into a control environment must be authorised, individually attributed, multi-factor authenticated, time-limited and recorded. This applies most strictly to vendors: remote support sessions are requested, approved, supervised while active, and closed afterwards. Standing, always-on vendor access is precisely how attackers get in.

Patch regularly — the OT way. Through the change process, in a maintenance window, tested first, with a rollback plan ready. Never ad hoc on a live system.

Monitor for anomalies. Unexpected commands, new devices, unusual traffic, logins at odd hours, a setting that changed without a work order.

The Polish incident of December 2025 shows how current this is. Attackers got in through internet-facing edge devices — routers and gateways sitting at the network perimeter, running out-of-date firmware. From there they deployed wiper malware and damaged remote terminal units across more than thirty renewable and combined heat and power sites. Supply held, but the operational damage was physical and real. The lesson is unglamorous: the forgotten box at the edge of the network, the one nobody has patched because nobody remembers owning it, is where these attacks start.

If it doesn't match known, authorised activity, treat it as an incident until proven otherwise.

## Slide 27 — Network Segmentation

Segmentation is the single most effective OT control we have. The principle is simple: IT and OT do not talk to each other directly.

Between them sits a DMZ — a buffer zone. Data that operations needs to share with the business is published into the DMZ, and business systems read it from there. Neither side reaches across.

Firewalls enforce that boundary, permitting only specific, approved traffic in a controlled direction. Everything else is denied by default. The most sensitive systems are isolated further still.

Two incidents show both sides of this. At Kudankulam in 2019, malware reached the administrative network — but the operational systems were genuinely separated, and the reactors were never affected. Segmentation did its job. Contrast that with Colonial Pipeline in 2021: the ransomware hit the IT side, not the control systems, yet the company shut the pipeline down anyway — because they could not be certain the separation would hold, and could not risk finding out. Good segmentation contains an incident. Uncertain segmentation forces you to shut down out of caution.

What this means for you is short but important. Never create your own bridge. Don't connect a laptop to both networks. Don't plug a phone, dongle or personal modem into an OT switch. Don't set up an unapproved wireless link or remote-access tool.

One shortcut can undo the entire design.

## Slide 28 — Incident Response for OT

If something looks wrong in an operational system, four steps.

Detect. Trust your instruments and your instincts. A reading that doesn't match physical reality. A command nobody issued. A device behaving oddly. An unfamiliar login. You know normal better than any tool does.

Isolate. Contain the affected system — but only through the approved procedure and with operational authority. In OT, disconnecting the wrong thing can do more damage than the attack. Safety and grid stability come first, so coordinate. Never act alone.

Report. Immediately, to the CISO office. The regulation gives us six hours to notify the authorities, and that clock starts when the incident is noticed.

Restore safely. Recovery is planned, sequenced and verified by the response team, once the system is confirmed clean.

Norsk Hydro is the model worth copying. They isolated fast, refused to pay, ran their plants manually where they could, and communicated openly with staff, customers and press throughout. It still cost them around seventy million dollars — but they kept operating and kept their reputation. The difference between a bad day and a catastrophe is usually the quality of the response, not the sophistication of the attack.

## Slide 29 — Your Role in OT Security

Three commitments to close this section.

Follow access controls. Use your own credentials, never anyone else's. Request only the access your role genuinely needs. Don't prop open doors to substations or control rooms, don't share badges, and never leave a logged-in operator console unattended. And when a colleague or contractor leaves, make sure their access goes with them — a water utility in the United States was sabotaged by a former employee whose remote login simply still worked.

Report anomalies. You are a detection layer no sensor replaces. An odd reading, an unexpected visitor, an engineer whose work order doesn't quite fit, a device that appeared without explanation — report it. Being wrong costs a phone call. Staying quiet can cost a great deal more.

Remember the Ukrainian operator watching his cursor move by itself. Every technical control in that utility had already been passed. He was the alarm. In our control rooms and substations, so are you.

Complete your cyber training. Not simply because a regulation requires it, but because it keeps the first two habits current as the threats change.

Reliable power depends on secure systems. Secure systems depend on you.

## Slide 30 — Key Takeaways

Let's close with what's worth carrying out of this session.

Think before you click. Verify the sender, hover over links, and treat urgency as a warning sign rather than an instruction. A single opened attachment started the first blackout in history caused by a cyberattack.

Use strong, unique passwords and turn on multi-factor authentication everywhere it's offered. One reused password on one forgotten account shut down a pipeline serving half a continent.

Protect your devices and your data. Lock your screen, use the VPN, never plug in unknown USB drives — one carried malware across an air gap and destroyed a thousand machines — and handle confidential information through approved channels only.

And report anything suspicious immediately, even, and especially, when you think the mistake was yours. In almost every case we've looked at, earlier reporting would have meant a smaller incident.

None of this requires you to become a security expert. It asks for a few seconds of attention at the right moments, and the confidence to ask a question when something feels off.

You now have both. Thank you for your time — and welcome to the team that keeps this organisation secure.
